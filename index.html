An Analysis of Observational Inquiries into Extrasolar Redshift and Universal Structure¶

By: Grayson Fuller¶

(1) Project Goals:¶

The following goals, having been judged of merit for the study of extrasolar redshift, are here proposed and enumerated.

  1. To investigate the cumulative observational data of extragalactic bodies, in order to model and represent both the present structure of the known universe, and its supposed time evolution. To quantify and visualize the the degree of cosmological redshift attributed to these bodies, and through such analysis, describe the relative motion of galactic clusters.
  2. To analyze both laboratory and telescopic measurements of graviational redshift, and thereby determine the relative densities of different sectors of the known universe.

As an expository note, it is important to clarify that current theories of physics identity three types of redshift: Doppler, Cosmological, and Gravitational (see 1.). Of these three, Cosmological and Graviational redshift are uniquely relavent when analzying physics on a universal scale. The first project goal is related to analysis of current data on cosmological redshift, which is the supposed shifting of light frequencies due to universal expansion (see 2.). In contrast, the second project goal is related to gravitational redshift, where the frequency of light is shifted when passing through a gravitational well, caused by a massive body (see 3.).

Currently one database has been identified as property suited for both these purposes: the NASA/IPAC Extragalatic Database (NED) NED. NED is a compendium of extragalatic objects and measurements of the electromagnetic spectrum coming from these objects. It combines measurements from a vast array of studies as well as international observatories and, thus, houses a stagering amount of data. While it is far from possible for us to run analysis on the whole of the NED database within the confines of Google Colab, it is possible to analayze a limited number of NED entries from some small portion of the night sky. For the present we have selected objects within the vicinity of the Messier 87 Galaxy, which is in the Virgo constellation (see 4.).

No other data sets have currently been decided upon; however, it is possible that we may use the laboratory measurements of graviational redshift gathered by Zheng et al. in 2023 (Zheng et al.).

(3) Questions for Analysis:¶

The following three questions may guide our future analysis:

  • Does the method used to measure redshift cause a reasonable differnce to the predicted redshift? In cases where mutliple types of redshift are present is the discrepancy amplified?
  • For certian stellar object types, is there greater redshift, of either type, and does the number of these stellar objects correlate with distance or certain sectors of space? Are there anomolous regions of space that differ significantly from redshift norms?
